# Airlines

## Snapshot
- About $800B+ in global revenue, ~30M flights a year, a 25K+ aircraft fleet, ~60M tonnes of cargo. Atlanta (ATL) is the busiest airport at ~94M passengers.
- **Three defining traits:** it is logistically complex (multi-country operations), capital intensive (aircraft) and very exposed to macro shocks (oil prices, recessions, pandemics).
- You've flown, so use your own experience as a sanity check on assumptions (seats per plane, fares, how full flights are).

## Players and business models
- **Legacy / network carriers** (Delta, United, American): hub-and-spoke networks, premium cabins, alliances.
- **Low-cost carriers (LCCs)** (Southwest, Ryanair, easyJet): point-to-point routes, a single aircraft type, no frills, unbundled fares.
- **Gulf carriers** (Emirates, Qatar, Etihad): long-haul connecting hubs.
- **Partners and enablers:** alliance and codeshare partners (e.g., Delta–KLM), airports, aircraft makers (Boeing, Airbus).

## Trends
| Past shifts | What's coming |
|---|---|
| 1980s deregulation brought new entrants and cheaper fares, followed by a wave of failures and mergers | Online and mobile booking and self-service |
| The rise of LCCs reset the price floor | Fuel-efficient aircraft (787, A350) that make longer, thinner routes viable |
| 9/11 brought new security and regulation | Automation (e.g., biometric check-in) |
| Consolidation (United–Continental, Alaska–Virgin America) | AI for revenue management and predictive maintenance |
| Growth in the Middle East and Asia | Sustainability (SAF/biofuels, offsets) |
| COVID collapse and recovery | Cybersecurity |

> **Example of under-investing in tech:** Southwest's December 2022 meltdown. Outdated crew-scheduling systems couldn't recover when winter storms hit at holiday peak.

## Money in
- **Passenger fares** are the core. Revenue = demand × price × capacity × load factor.
- **Cargo** depends on trade volumes and freight rates. Belly cargo on passenger flights is close to free capacity.
- **Ancillaries**: bags, seat selection, upgrades, onboard sales. This is the "unbundling" lever.
- **Loyalty programs**: airlines sell miles to credit-card and hotel partners, which is often a very high-margin business.
- **Premium cabins** earn far more revenue per square foot of cabin than economy, especially on long-haul.
- **Codeshare and interline deals** extend network reach without flying the plane yourself.

## Money out
1. **Fuel**: often the #1 or #2 cost. It's volatile, so airlines hedge it.
2. **Labor**: heavily unionized. Example: Delta's recent pilot contract carried $800M+ in one-time costs plus higher ongoing pay.
3. **Aircraft**: leases or ownership plus depreciation, and maintenance (MRO). Depends on fleet age and utilization.
4. **Airport and navigation fees**: landing, gate, ATC and security.
5. **Distribution and sales**: GDS fees and OTA commissions.
6. **Other**: insurance, IT and overhead.

## Metrics (km and mile versions are interchangeable, e.g., RPK vs. RPM)
| Metric | Formula / meaning |
|---|---|
| **ASK** (available seat km) | Seats × km flown. A measure of **capacity** |
| **RPK** (revenue passenger km) | Paying passengers × km flown. A measure of **traffic** |
| **Load factor** | RPK ÷ ASK. How full the planes are |
| **RASK** | Revenue ÷ ASK (sometimes passenger revenue only, called PRASK) |
| **CASK** | Operating cost ÷ ASK. Often quoted "ex-fuel" to strip out volatility |
| **RASK − CASK** | Unit profit per seat-km. **This spread is the profitability story** |
| **OTP** (on-time performance) | % of flights within 15 minutes of schedule |

## Case angles
- **Pricing** (e.g., premium economy): segment the customers, then weigh willingness to pay, cannibalization of economy and business, and the cabin reconfiguration cost.
- **New route / market entry**: demand (O&D traffic), competition, slot and gate access, aircraft fit, and route-level RASK vs. CASK.
- **Revenue growth**: ancillaries, loyalty partnerships, premium mix, cargo.
- **Profit decline**: split it into RASK vs. CASK first, then fuel vs. ex-fuel.
