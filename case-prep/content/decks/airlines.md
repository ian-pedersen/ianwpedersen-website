## Q: What are the three defining traits of the airline industry?
@tags overview
1. **Logistically complex**: multi-country operations, crews, fleets, slots.
2. **Capital intensive**: aircraft are bought or leased for decades.
3. **Exposed to macro shocks**: oil prices, recessions, pandemics.

## Q: Airlines: what are ASK and RPK?
@tags metric, formula
- **ASK** (available seat km) = seats × km flown. Measures **capacity**.
- **RPK** (revenue passenger km) = paying passengers × km flown. Measures **traffic**.

Mile versions (ASM, RPM) are interchangeable.

## Q: Airlines: how do you calculate load factor?
@tags metric, formula
**Load factor = RPK ÷ ASK**. It shows how full the planes are.

## Q: Airlines: define RASK and CASK. Which spread tells the profitability story?
@tags metric, formula
- **RASK** = revenue ÷ ASK (PRASK if passenger revenue only)
- **CASK** = operating cost ÷ ASK
- **RASK − CASK** = unit profit per seat-km. That spread is the profitability story.

## Q: Why do analysts quote CASK "ex-fuel"?
@tags metric, quiz
Fuel is large and volatile (and partly hedged). Stripping it out shows whether the airline's **controllable** unit costs (labor, maintenance, fees, overhead) are improving.

## Q: Airlines: what are the main revenue streams?
@tags revenue
- **Passenger fares**: demand × price × capacity × load factor
- **Cargo**: belly cargo on passenger flights is close to free capacity
- **Ancillaries**: bags, seats, upgrades (the unbundling lever)
- **Loyalty**: selling miles to card and hotel partners, very high margin
- **Premium cabins**: far more revenue per sq ft, especially long-haul
- **Codeshare / interline**: network reach without flying the plane

## Q: Airlines: what are the main cost drivers, roughly in order?
@tags cost
1. **Fuel**: #1 or #2 cost, volatile, hedged
2. **Labor**: heavily unionized
3. **Aircraft**: leases or ownership, depreciation, maintenance (MRO)
4. **Airport and navigation fees**: landing, gate, ATC, security
5. **Distribution**: GDS fees, OTA commissions
6. **Other**: insurance, IT, overhead

## Q: Name the main airline business models and an example of each.
@tags players
- **Legacy / network**: hub-and-spoke, premium cabins, alliances (Delta, United, American)
- **Low-cost carriers**: point-to-point, one aircraft type, unbundled fares (Southwest, Ryanair)
- **Gulf carriers**: long-haul connecting hubs (Emirates, Qatar)

## Q: How does a low-cost carrier keep its CASK below a legacy carrier's?
@tags cost, quiz
- One aircraft type: cheaper training, maintenance and spares
- Point-to-point routes and fast turns: higher aircraft utilization
- Denser seating, no premium cabin
- Secondary airports with lower fees
- Direct distribution (fewer GDS and OTA fees)

## Q: Load factor rose, but profit fell. Give two possible explanations.
@tags quiz, case-angle
- **Yield fell**: planes were filled by discounting, so RASK dropped.
- **Costs rose faster**: fuel, labor contracts or fees pushed CASK up.
- Mix shifted to shorter or lower-fare routes.

## Q: Why is a loyalty program attractive to a credit-card partner, and why is it so profitable for the airline?
@tags revenue, quiz
- **Card partner**: miles are a sticky reward that wins and keeps high-spending cardholders.
- **Airline**: it sells miles for cash up front, and the cost to redeem them (often an otherwise empty seat) is low. Some miles are never redeemed.

## Q: Airline profit is declining. How do you start the structure?
@tags case-angle
Split it into **RASK vs. CASK** first.
- RASK: yield (price) vs. load factor, ancillaries, cargo, mix
- CASK: **fuel vs. ex-fuel**, then labor, aircraft, fees, distribution

## Q: Airline new route decision: what do you look at?
@tags case-angle
- Demand: O&D traffic, segments (business vs. leisure)
- Competition on the route and its fares
- Slot and gate access
- Aircraft fit (range, size)
- Route-level **RASK vs. CASK**, and network feed effects
